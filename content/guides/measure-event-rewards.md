# How to Measure Event Rewards Sponsors Will Want to Renew

Sponsors renew what they can measure. How to track viewed, claimed and redeemed rewards at events and build a recap partners trust.

Renewal conversations are won or lost on the recap. A sponsor who can see clearly what their money did is a sponsor who comes back, often with a bigger budget. A sponsor who gets an attendance number and a photo gallery starts shopping around. Here's how to measure event rewards so the recap makes the case for you.

### Why traditional event metrics fall short

Attendance tells a sponsor how many people were on site, not how many noticed them. Foot traffic counts at a booth are estimates. Social mentions are real but hard to tie to anything. None of these show that a specific person engaged with a specific offer and then did something because of it.

Rewards change that, because each one creates a clear trail of actions you can count.

### The funnel: viewed, claimed, redeemed

Track every reward through three stages.

**Viewed** is how many attendees saw the reward. It measures placement and reach.

**Claimed** is how many chose to take it. It measures how appealing the offer is to the people who saw it.

**Redeemed** is how many actually used it, at the bar, the stand or the partner's own store. It measures real outcomes.

Report all three for every reward. A sponsor who sees the drop off between each stage understands the result far better than one who sees a single big number.

### Break it down by placement and time

The most useful recaps slice the funnel two ways.

**By placement.** Which claim points got seen, and which converted? If the sticker at the main bar drove most claims and the sign by the entrance barely registered, that's valuable for next year's layout.

**By time.** When did people claim and redeem? Rewards tied to a window, like early arrival or a quiet set, should show a clear spike inside that window. That spike is your evidence that the reward changed behavior, not just that people happened to be around.

### Reading the results

**High views, low claims.** The offer isn't a fit for the crowd, or the rules are unclear. Try a different reward or simpler wording.

**High claims, low redemptions.** Something gets in the way between interest and use: long lines, confused staff, a redemption point that's hard to find. Fix the operation before blaming the offer.

**Low views.** It's a placement problem. Move claim points to where people actually stop.

### What to expect from a first event

Treat the first event as a baseline, not a verdict. You'll learn which placements work at your venue, which windows people respond to, and how quickly staff get comfortable. Change one variable at a time on the next event so you can tell what made the difference. Keep notes on what you tried, because they become your pitch.

Set expectations with sponsors up front. Promise the funnel, the breakdowns and an honest read of what you learned, rather than a specific number. Partners appreciate a producer who reports clearly, even when a result is modest.

### Keep it aggregate

You don't need names to prove value. Totals by reward, placement and time tell a sponsor everything they need, and they keep you out of the business of collecting and storing personal data. It also makes attendees more willing to take part, which grows every number in the funnel.

### Building the recap

A good recap fits on one page: the reward, the audience it targeted, the window, viewed, claimed and redeemed totals, the best and worst placements, and what you'd change next time. Send it within a week while the event is still fresh.

### Plan a pilot

Choose one audience, one reward and one result to measure. Set the budget and eligibility rules before launch. Tell participants which information you need and share results in aggregate where possible.
