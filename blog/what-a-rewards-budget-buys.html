What a rewards budget actually buys a retail brand

When a brand's finance lead first looks at funding rewards, the question is usually some version of: what am I buying? With paid social, the answer is impressions and clicks. With a rewards budget, it's less obvious, and I think that's why a lot of brands either overspend or never start.

So let me be concrete about what a rewards budget buys, what it doesn't, and how I'd size one for a retail or ecommerce brand.

First, how the money moves

On Perkalate, brands fund rewards, and those rewards reach shoppers verified by loyalty membership or tier plus merchant, category or card spend in the last 90 days. Brands pay only for the rewards they fund. It's free to start, so there's no platform commitment to justify before you've seen anything.

That's a different shape from most marketing spend. With an ad, you pay for the chance to be seen. With a funded reward, the budget is the reward itself, aimed at a group of shoppers you defined.

What it buys: four things

1. A defined audience. You pick the signals. Two or three of them stacked, such as Sephora Beauty Insiders at VIB or Rouge whose cards also show beauty category spend in the last 90 days. You know who the reward is for before you spend anything.

2. A reason to buy now. A new shopper has no reason to try you this week rather than next year. A reward worth having gives them one. Antavo's 2026 consumer data found 70.8% of consumers are motivated by savings and 46.3% see free products or services as a sign of a mature program (Antavo).

3. A first purchase you can measure. Because you know what you funded and who it was for, you can count the first-time buyers it produced and work out a cost per new customer.

4. An entry point into your own program. Once someone buys, your email, SMS and loyalty program take over. That's where the long-term value shows up. At Applied Nutrition, members are worth 215% more in lifetime value than non-members (Smile.io).

What it doesn't buy

A rewards budget won't buy reach for its own sake. If your goal is to get your logo in front of as many people as possible, paid social and out-of-home are built for that.

It also won't fix a weak product or a bad first experience. A reward gets someone to try you. Whether they come back is on you.

And it doesn't make attribution easy. Antavo found 31.6% of loyalty program owners struggle to tell which purchases happened because of the program (Antavo). Keep tests clean so you can read them.

The comparison that matters

The number I'd put a rewards budget against is your own paid social cost per acquisition.

For context, Triple Whale's benchmarks across more than 40,000 brands put median Meta cost per acquisition at $38.99, up 3.14% year over year, with median CPM up 13.24% to $15.06 (Triple Whale). By category, Beauty's median CPA is $39.31 and Apparel & Accessories is $36.98. Your number may be higher or lower. Use yours.

Then do the simple math: what does it cost you to get one new customer through a funded reward? If that number is lower than your paid social CPA, and those customers come back at a similar or better rate, the rewards budget is earning its place.

How I'd size a first budget

Here's the approach I'd take, with no made-up conversion rates.

Step 1: pick the reward value. Something a shopper would notice. Look at your average order value and margin and decide what you can afford to give a first-time buyer.

Step 2: pick how many rewards to fund. Enough to learn something, small enough that a bad result doesn't hurt. Total budget is reward value times rewards funded.

Step 3: write down your break-even. Using your own margin, how many first purchases do you need from that budget to beat your paid social cost per acquisition? That's your target.

Step 4: run one segment. Two or three stacked signals. Hold everything else steady.

Step 5: read the results at 30, 60 and 90 days. First purchase cost tells you whether it worked. Repeat purchases tell you whether it's worth scaling.

Why tight beats broad

It's tempting to spread a rewards budget wide to see more activity. I'd do the opposite. Value is concentrated. At Live Bearded, the top 8% of loyalty members generated 90% of revenue with a loyalty discount code over the past 12 months (Smile.io). If your best customers look like that, a tightly stacked segment that finds more people like them is worth more than a broad one that finds a lot of casual browsers.

Where the budget comes from

Most brands I talk to don't create a new line item. They move a slice of their paid social test budget, or a slice of their promotions budget. Antavo found 59.8% of marketers would shift more budget from short-term promotions into loyalty if they could (Antavo). A funded reward for a verified new shopper is a natural place to put some of that.

The takeaway

A rewards budget buys a defined audience, a reason to buy now, a measurable first purchase and a way into your own program. Size it small, set your break-even in advance and compare it with what you already pay for a customer.
