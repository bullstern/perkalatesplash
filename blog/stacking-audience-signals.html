One signal isn't enough: stacking audience signals to find your best customers

Every brand I talk to has a version of the same targeting problem. They know roughly who their best customer is. They can describe her. What they can't do is find more people like her without paying to show ads to a lot of people who aren't.

The fix I keep coming back to is simple to say and easy to get wrong: don't target on one signal. Stack two or three, and make at least one of them recent.

Why a single signal misleads you

Take loyalty tier on its own. It sounds like the best signal there is. Someone who's Rouge at Sephora spent at least $1,000 on Sephora merchandise in a calendar year. But read the terms closely: Rouge status is good for the year the member qualifies and the following full calendar year (Sephora). Ulta's Platinum and Diamond tiers work the same way, valid through Dec. 31 of the following year (Ulta). lululemon's levels do too (lululemon).

So a tier tells you someone spent a lot at some point in a window that can stretch close to two years. It doesn't tell you what they're doing this quarter. A Rouge member who qualified early last year and moved on is still Rouge today.

Spend on its own has the opposite problem. A card showing beauty category spend in the last 90 days tells you someone is buying beauty right now. It doesn't tell you whether that was a one-off gift or a habit, or whether they buy at the price point you sell at.

Each signal answers one question. Neither answers the question you actually care about, which is: is this person likely to love what I sell, and are they buying in my category now?

How stacking works

Stacking means requiring a shopper to match more than one signal before they're in your audience. Each signal you add takes out people who match on paper but aren't a real fit.

On Perkalate, the signals are deliberately limited to two kinds:

1. Loyalty membership or tier. Is the shopper a member of a given program, and at what level? 2. Merchant, category or card spend in the last 90 days. Where have they spent recently, in what categories, on what kind of card?

That's it. Neither one is about an individual item someone bought, and I think that's the right call for both privacy and accuracy. A single product purchase is a noisy signal. A pattern of spend in a category over 90 days is a much better read on behavior.

A good stack usually looks like this: one signal that says this person values the category enough to earn status in it, plus one or two that say and they're actively spending now.

Three stacks, built the right way

Here's what that looks like in practice for LA brands.

A clean beauty brand in Silver Lake. Sephora Beauty Insiders at VIB or Rouge, whose cards also show beauty category spend in the last 90 days. The tier says they invest in beauty. The 90-day spend says they still do.

A contemporary fashion label selling online. Nordy Club members at Ambassador status or higher (Nordstrom), whose cards show apparel category spend and luxury retail merchant spend in the last 90 days. Three signals: status, category and price point.

A wellness brand selling supplements and recovery tools. lululemon members at Collective Plus or Pinnacle, which unlock at $500 and $1,000 in yearly spend (lululemon), whose cards show fitness studio spend in the last 90 days.

Notice what's not in there. No "bought a specific serum," no "purchased running shoes in size 8." Just membership or tier plus recent spend.

Why recency matters so much now

The case for stacking gets stronger when you look at how concentrated customer value is.

At Live Bearded, the top VIP tier makes up 8% of loyalty members but generated 90% of revenue with a loyalty discount code in the past 12 months (Smile.io). At Henry's House of Coffee, customers in the top Master tier spend 519% more than the base tier (Smile.io). Your best customers aren't a little better than average. They're in a different league.

At the same time, the 2026 Bond Loyalty Report describes wallet consolidation: consumer dollars concentrating among fewer preferred brands. If the shoppers you want are narrowing down where they spend, you need to know where they're spending now, not where they spent last year.

That's what the 90-day signal gives you. The tier finds people with the right habits. Recent spend tells you the habit is still live.

Common mistakes

Stacking too much. Four or five signals can shrink an audience to almost nobody. Start with two, add a third only if the segment is clearly too broad.

Stacking signals that say the same thing. Two beauty loyalty tiers don't tell you much more than one. Mix a tier signal with a spend signal.

Skipping the hypothesis. Write down why this group should like you. If you can't say it in a sentence, the segment isn't ready.

Calling it after one try. A segment that doesn't respond to one reward might respond to a different one. Change one thing at a time.

Start with the customer you already know

The best place to build a stack is your own customer list. Look at your top buyers and ask what else they're members of and where else they shop. Then turn that into two or three signals.
